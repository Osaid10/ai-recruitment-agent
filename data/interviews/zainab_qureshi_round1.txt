Interview: Senior Python Engineer - Round 1 (Technical + Role Fit)
Date: 12 August 2026, 45 minutes
Panel: Ayesha Raza (Engineering Manager), Daniyal Ahmed (Staff Engineer)
Candidate: Zainab Qureshi

---

Ayesha: Thanks for making the time. Let's start with the scheduling service you
own now. Walk us through what it actually does.

Candidate: It handles appointment booking for the telehealth product. A patient
picks a doctor, sees available slots, books one. Sounds simple, but the
availability calculation is the hard part, because a doctor's availability is a
function of their working hours, their existing bookings, their leave, and the
clinic's rules, and all of that changes constantly. It's FastAPI on Postgres,
about 200,000 bookings a month.

Daniyal: You mentioned in your CV that you took p95 on the availability endpoint
from 1.9 seconds to 210 milliseconds. What was the actual problem?

Candidate: It was an N+1 in disguise. For each candidate slot in the requested
window we were running a query to check whether it collided with an existing
booking. For a two-week window at fifteen-minute granularity that's several
hundred queries per request. I replaced it with one query that pulls all the
bookings in the window as a range, and then did the collision check in memory.
The index was the other half of it - we had an index on doctor_id but not on
(doctor_id, starts_at), so the range scan was reading far more rows than it
needed.

Daniyal: Did you consider caching instead?

Candidate: I did, and I decided against it, at least initially. Availability
changes every time anyone books, so the invalidation would have been the hard
part, and I'd rather not add a cache to hide a query I hadn't understood yet.
Once the query was fast the cache wasn't worth the complexity. If we grow ten
times I'd revisit it, and at that point I'd probably cache the doctor's working
pattern, which changes rarely, rather than the computed availability.

Ayesha: Tell us about the database migration - splitting the shared database
into per-service schemas. That's the kind of project that goes badly.

Candidate: It does. We ran it over about six weeks. The approach was dual
writing. First we created the new schema and started writing to both, with the
old one still the source of truth. Then we backfilled history and wrote a
reconciliation job that compared the two and alerted on any drift. We let that
run for about ten days until the drift was consistently zero. Then we flipped
reads over one endpoint at a time, and only after all of them were on the new
schema for a week did we stop writing to the old one.

Daniyal: Did anything go wrong?

Candidate: Yes. The reconciliation job found a class of mismatch we hadn't
expected, around appointments that were cancelled and rebooked in the same
transaction. The old code was relying on an implicit ordering that didn't hold
in the new schema. It took about three days to find, and honestly we only found
it because of the reconciliation job. If we'd done a straight cutover we'd have
shipped a data bug and not known for weeks.

Ayesha: You've got two mid-level engineers reporting into your design review.
How do you handle it when someone brings you a design you think is wrong?

Candidate: I try to ask what problem it solves before I say anything about the
design, because usually if I disagree it's because I've misunderstood the
constraint. If I still disagree after that, I'll say so directly and say why,
but I try to make it about the tradeoff rather than about the design being
wrong. And if it's reversible I'd usually rather let them ship it and see. The
ones I push back hard on are the decisions that are expensive to undo - schema
choices, public API shapes.

Daniyal: Let's talk about the AWS side. What have you actually run in production?

Candidate: ECS for services, RDS for Postgres, S3 for stored documents. I've
written the Terraform for all of that. I have not run Kubernetes in production -
we looked at EKS and decided ECS was enough for our size. I've used Kubernetes
in a previous side project but I wouldn't claim production depth there.

Ayesha: You took a break between 2023 and 2024. Anything you'd want to tell us
about coming back?

Candidate: It was family caregiving, so it was a full stop rather than a
sabbatical. I kept the open-source library maintained through it, mostly because
it was a couple of hours a week and it kept me current. Coming back, the first
month was slower than I expected - not the code, the context. Learning a new
codebase and a new domain at the same time. After that it was fine.

Daniyal: One more. How do you decide what to monitor?

Candidate: I start from what a user would notice. For the booking service that's
"can I see slots" and "did my booking go through", so those become the two SLOs,
and everything else is diagnostic. I'm wary of alerting on CPU or memory, because
those page you for things that aren't user-visible and people learn to ignore
them. What I have not done well is error budgets - we track the SLOs but we don't
really use them to make release decisions, and I think we should.

Ayesha: Anything you want to ask us?

Candidate: What does on-call look like here, and who owns the services out of
hours? And separately - the JD mentions taking data science models to production.
Is that a real part of the job or aspirational? I ask because I've handed models
off to a platform team before and I've been on the receiving end, and they're
very different jobs.

Ayesha: Fair question. It's real, it's about a quarter of the work.

Candidate: Then I'd want to know how the models arrive - as a notebook, as a
container, as something in between. That difference is most of the effort.

Daniyal: That's the right question to ask. We'll follow up.
