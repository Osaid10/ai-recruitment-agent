Interview: Senior Python Engineer - Round 1 (Technical + Role Fit)
Date: 12 August 2026, 45 minutes
Panel: Ayesha Raza (Engineering Manager), Daniyal Ahmed (Staff Engineer)
Candidate: Ahmed Nadeem

Ayesha: Let's start with the idempotency work you mentioned on your CV. What was
the problem?

Candidate: Duplicate charges. A customer's app would retry a payment request on
a timeout, and we'd process it twice. It was our single biggest support category.

Daniyal: How did you fix it?

Candidate: Idempotency keys. The client sends a key with the request, we store it
with the result, and if we see the same key again we return the stored result
instead of processing again.

Daniyal: What's the tricky part of that?

Candidate: Making sure the key and the charge are written atomically. If you
write the key first and then the charge fails, you've locked out a legitimate
retry. We put them in the same transaction.

Daniyal: What about two concurrent requests with the same key arriving at once?

Candidate: We use a unique constraint on the key, so the second one fails to
insert and we return the first result.

Daniyal: What if the first is still in flight and hasn't got a result yet?

Candidate: Then... I think the second one would get a conflict error and the
client would retry. I'd have to look at the code to be sure. It's been a while
since I was in that part of the system.

Ayesha: The monolith-to-services migration. How long did it take and what was
the approach?

Candidate: About nine months. Strangler pattern - we put a routing layer in
front and moved endpoints across one at a time.

Ayesha: What went wrong?

Candidate: Honestly it went pretty smoothly. The routing layer meant we could
move things gradually and roll back easily. I'd say the hardest part was
convincing people it was worth doing.

Ayesha: Nothing broke at all?

Candidate: There were the usual small things. Nothing I'd call a major incident.

Daniyal: You listed Kubernetes and EKS. What do you run on it?

Candidate: The services run on EKS. I wrote the Helm charts and the deployment
pipeline. Day to day I'm not in the cluster much - we have a platform team who
handle the infrastructure side.

Daniyal: If a pod is in CrashLoopBackOff, what do you do?

Candidate: Check the logs with kubectl logs, look at the events with describe.
Usually it's a config or a missing secret.

Daniyal: And if the logs are empty?

Candidate: Then it's failing before the app starts. Could be the image, could be
the command. I'd check the previous container's logs with --previous.

Ayesha: You mentor two engineers. Tell us about a time that was difficult.

Candidate: One of them was writing very long functions and I kept flagging it in
review. Eventually I sat down with them and we refactored one together, and it
clicked.

Ayesha: What made it click?

Candidate: I think seeing it done rather than being told.

Ayesha: How do you know it stuck?

Candidate: Their reviews got shorter.

Daniyal: The p99 latency work - 840ms to 190ms. What did you measure first?

Candidate: We had traces, so I looked at the span breakdown and the database
time was dominant. From there it was the N+1 and the missing indexes.

Daniyal: How did you verify the indexes were the right ones?

Candidate: EXPLAIN ANALYZE before and after.

Daniyal: Any downside to adding them?

Candidate: Write cost, mostly. The tables are read-heavy so it was a clear win.

Candidate: What does the team structure look like, and what's the tech stack
